DoRobot Studio · UX Concept · v2 · whole-body

One robot, two teachers

v1 turned the LeRobot CLI into an operator console for arms. Adding legs does not add a screen — it adds a second way of learning, with a different teacher, a different unit of work, and a different failure mode. This is the design for keeping both in one window without pretending they are the same thing.

01

What changes when the robot has legs

the thesis

An arm is a manipulator bolted to a table. A humanoid is a manipulator standing on something that can fall over — and the two halves are not independent.

Reaching forward moves the centre of mass forward. Lifting a 2 kg box changes the model the legs were trained against. Every good thing the arms do is a disturbance the legs have to reject. That coupling is the entire design problem, and it is why bolting a training tab next to a recorder does not produce a whole-body studio — it produces two apps sharing a title bar.

The second problem is that the two halves are taught in incompatible ways. The arms learn from a human in the room, one watchable episode at a time. The legs learn from a reward function on a GPU, ten thousand environments at a time. Almost every UX decision below follows from this table.

v1's laws were written for the left column. Three of them need company before they survive the right one.

02

Three more laws

08 total

The original five still hold verbatim. These three exist because simulation introduces artefacts that outlive the session that made them, and because a humanoid can hurt itself.

LAW 06

Provenance is part of the artifact

A policy names the dataset, scene, reward weights, seed and validation that produced it. A checkpoint you cannot trace is a checkpoint you cannot ship — and after a week of runs, memory is not traceability.

LAW 07

Earn the real robot

Nothing drives hardware until it has cleared training sim, then a second simulator. The ladder is a visible gate with rungs a policy has or has not cleared, not a convention someone remembers to follow.

LAW 08

Long work is abandonable

Any run reports progress in its own unit — env-steps, not a spinner — and can be stopped, resumed and compared against its siblings. A six-hour job you cannot judge at minute ten is a six-hour job you will not stop.

Law 05 — one panel vocabulary — does the heaviest lifting in v2. A reward curve, a joint trace and a divergence chart are the same widget with different series. A sim viewport and the calibration mirror are the same RobotView. That is what keeps eight destinations from feeling like eight products.

03

Two production lines, one robot

information architecture

v1's nav was the data lifecycle, left to right. v2 has two lifecycles that converge, so the nav gains bands rather than more undifferentiated items.

Capture · real
  • Hardwarecalibrate, profile the rig
  • Recorddemonstrate the task
  • Playcurate what was captured
Learn · sim + data
  • Sceneauthor the training environment
  • Trainruns, curves, checkpoints
Deploy · whole body
  • Composebind upper + lower into one controller
  • Evalthe promotion ladder to hardware

Library sits above the bands as home, and grows from one shelf to three: Datasets (what the arms were shown), Scenes (what the legs were trained in), Policies (what came out). Same card vocabulary, three nouns.

Why Train is one destination, not two

The strongest temptation is a separate "RL" section. It is wrong. Imitation-from-dataset and reinforcement-in-sim differ in what they consume, but the operator's job is identical in both: launch a run, decide at minute ten whether it is learning, promote a checkpoint or kill it. One surface with two run kinds keeps that judgement in one place — and makes the honest comparison "which of my seven runs actually got better" possible at all.

What binds the halves

v1 had two binding objects: the rig profile and the panel set. v2 adds a third and it is load-bearing: the rig profile also parameterises the simulation. The URDF, joint limits and calibrated ranges produced by the Hardware wizard are what the Scene instantiates. Calibrate once; the sim inherits the robot you actually own, not the one in the manufacturer's file.

04

The new screens

design intent

Scene

Newsim authoring

Turn a rig profile and a sentence about what "good" means into a training environment you can look at before spending GPU-hours in it.

Kills

Editing a Python config dict, launching, waiting twenty minutes, and discovering the reward rewarded standing still. Also kills "what did domain randomization actually do?" — currently answerable only by reading code.

Anatomy

  • Robot slot — populated from the rig profile. The sim uses your calibrated joint limits, so a policy is not trained against ranges the physical arm does not have.
  • Reward composer — named terms with weights. Naming them is what makes per-term diagnosis possible during training; an unnamed scalar is undebuggable by construction.
  • Randomization ranges with a sample action that renders eight draws side by side. Domain randomization becomes something you looked at rather than something you configured.
  • Observation / action contract — an explicit table of what the policy sees and emits. This is the document sim-to-real disputes are settled with.
  • Terrain — flat, slope, stairs, rough — each with the curriculum thresholds that promote between them.

Build notes

  • The preview is the existing RobotView against the same URDF the calibration mirror uses — no new 3D code, and it proves the profile round-trips before a run starts.
  • Scene serialises to the config zealot's env builder already consumes; the UI is an editor for that file, never a second source of truth.
  • Sampling draws are computed by the sim, not the UI — the UI requests n and renders what comes back.

Train

Newtwo run kinds

Launch a run, judge at minute ten whether it is learning, and promote or kill it — without a browser tab, an ssh session, or a log tail.

Kills

TensorBoard in another window, tail -f in a third, and the recurring question "is 71k steps/s good, or is my GPU idle?" Also kills the graveyard of unnamed checkpoint directories that no one dares delete.

Anatomy

  • Run header — env count, throughput, elapsed, and progress in env-steps. Law 08: the unit is the work, not the clock.
  • Per-term reward curves — one line per named Scene term. When total reward rises because feet_air_time is being gamed while track_lin_vel is flat, that is visible rather than inferred.
  • Live environment grid — a contact sheet of running envs. Falls are tinted. This is the fastest "is it learning to walk or learning to cheat" signal available anywhere in the stack.
  • Termination histogram — fell / timeout / success, over time. A rising fall rate with rising reward is the classic reward-hacking fingerprint.
  • Checkpoint rail — auto-saved, scored, one click to promote into Policies with provenance attached (Law 06).
  • Imitation runs use the identical frame: dataset instead of scene, loss instead of reward, validation episodes instead of env grid. Same verbs, same buttons.

Build notes

  • The UI never links the trainer. A run is a job; zealot-rl emits a metrics stream and the UI is a consumer — the same posture as v1's "no ML in the UI process".
  • Curves are TimeSeriesPlot in small-multiple mode — already needed for Eval's divergence stack. Build once, used by both.
  • The env grid is the one genuinely new render path: n viewports onto one sim. Cheapest honest version is a strided readback of n env root states into n small RobotViews.
  • Throughput and GPU occupancy come from the trainer, displayed as a health signal — a collapsed steps/s is how you learn the loop fell off the GPU.

Compose

Newthe whole-body screen

Bind an upper-body policy learned from demonstration to a lower-body policy learned in simulation, and see how they fight before either touches hardware.

Kills

Nothing — and that is the point. There is no tool for this today. You write an arbiter in Python, run it, and find out on the floor. This screen is the one place the product is not competing with an existing workflow but supplying a missing one.

Anatomy

  • Ownership map — every joint coloured by which policy commands it. Contested joints are amber and must be resolved explicitly; the screen will not let you ship an ambiguous binding.
  • Handoff contract — what the upper policy publishes (end-effector targets, gripper) and what the lower consumes (a disturbance budget). Stating this is what makes the two independently replaceable.
  • Balance ledger — centre-of-mass projection against the support polygon, with margin over time and the payload the legs were trained to expect. Worst margin during the reach is the number that predicts a fall, and no other screen in the product can compute it.
  • Co-simulation preview — run the composed controller in nexus: arms performing the demonstrated task, legs stabilising. Failures are classified — fell, dropped, recovered, succeeded — not just counted.
  • Arbiter — priority or learned blending on the contested set, versioned with the binding.

Build notes

  • Composition is a dora graph — VLA node, locomotion node, arbiter node — which is the shape the bridge already assumes. The screen edits and inspects that graph; it does not host inference.
  • Support polygon comes from foot contacts in sim; CoM from the same articulated state nexus already integrates. Both are readbacks, not new physics.
  • A binding is itself an artifact with provenance: two parent policies, an arbiter config, a co-sim result. It appears in Library as a policy of kind whole-body.

Eval

Extends v1now a gate

v1's Eval watched a policy drive the rig. v2's Eval decides whether it is allowed to.

A humanoid that falls damages itself and whatever it is holding. Law 07 turns evaluation from a screen you visit into a ladder a policy climbs, with each rung recorded on the policy card.

Success rate across the env population it was trained in. Cheap, and proves only that it learned something. Passing is necessary and means little.

The same policy in a different engine. This is where physics overfit surfaces: a gait that exploited the training solver's contact model degrades here, and that is exactly what you want to learn indoors.

What v1's Eval gains

  • Fall taxonomy replaces a binary success flag: fell, recovered, task failed but stayed upright, succeeded. For a humanoid these are four different engineering problems.
  • Balance trace alongside the joint divergence stack — the whole-body failure is usually a balance failure that a per-joint view will not show.
  • Sim-versus-real overlay — the same episode as predicted in sim and as executed on hardware, on one time axis. That difference is the sim-to-real gap, made into an image instead of a feeling.

Library, Hardware, Record, Play

Extends v1
  • Library gains the Scenes and Policies shelves, and policy cards carry the ladder state from Eval — so "what is safe to run" is answered at the home screen.
  • Hardware gains a second output: alongside the rig profile it now emits the sim robot definition. One calibration, two consumers.
  • Record gains bimanual layout and a standing mode where the demonstration is captured while the legs hold a balance controller — because a humanoid demo recorded with locked legs teaches the arms a disturbance profile the real robot will never see.
  • Play gains the balance trace as a curatable channel: a take where the robot nearly fell is a bad take even if the manipulation succeeded.
05

The flow that matters

arms + legs → one robot

The capture and curation loops from v1 are unchanged. Two new flows carry v2, and the second is the one the whole design exists to make possible.

Teach the legs

Instantiate the scene from the rig profile.

The robot you calibrated is the robot that gets simulated — same URDF, same joint limits.

Compose the reward as named terms; sample the randomization.

Eight draws render side by side. You have now seen the distribution you are about to train against.

Launch, then judge at minute ten.

Per-term curves plus the env grid. Reward climbing while falls climb is a kill, not a wait.

Promote a checkpoint.

It lands in Policies with scene, reward weights and seed attached.

Clear rungs 01 and 02.

Cross-sim in MuJoCo before anything is allowed near hardware.

Make it one robot

Pick an upper policy and a lower policy in Compose.

Ownership map fills in; the torso lights amber as contested.

Resolve the contested set and state the contract.

Upper publishes end-effector targets; lower consumes a disturbance budget. Explicit, versioned, replaceable.

Co-simulate the real task.

Arms run the demonstrated motion while legs stabilise. Watch the CoM margin during the reach — this is the number that predicts the fall.

Tighten whichever half is wrong.

Margin too thin can mean the legs need the payload in their randomization, or the demonstration was recorded with locked legs. The ledger distinguishes them.

Promote the binding, climb the ladder.

The whole-body controller is itself an artifact with parents, and it earns hardware the same way its halves did.

Implementation map

against this workspace

v1 leaned on machinery already in the repository. v2 leans on machinery that exists in someone else's repository — which is a different risk, and worth naming precisely.

CapabilityStatusWork required
GPU physicsExternalnexus, via zealot. Runs as a job; the UI never links it.
PPO / GAE / AdamExternalzealot-rl. Consume its metric stream; no trainer in-process.
Cross-sim gateExternalMuJoCo is already zealot's reference validator — wrap it as rung 02.
3D viewport, joints from stateExistsRobotView serves scene preview, env grid cells and co-sim.
Small-multiple plotsPartialBuilt for Play's per-joint lanes; reused for reward terms and divergence.
Panel shell, grid, persistenceExistsReuse across three new surfaces (makepad-app-shell).
Job runner + metric streamNewLaunch, stream, stop, resume. Shared by both run kinds.
Policy registryNewManifest with provenance and ladder state. Mirrors the tag-sidecar pattern.
Scene authoringNewEditor over zealot's env config; reward terms, randomization, curriculum.
Environment gridNewStrided readback of n env states into n viewports.
Whole-body arbiterNewThe keystone. dora graph, contract, CoM/support-polygon readout.
VLA trainingNewJob runner over an existing trainer; not ours to write.

Sequencing

Policy registry first. It is the cheapest item and everything else produces or consumes its artifacts; built early, it stops the checkpoint graveyard forming. Then the job runner and Train, because a run you can watch is immediately useful even with Scene still a config file. Then Scene, then Compose. Eval's ladder can be built a rung at a time and is useful from rung 01.

Compose is deliberately last. It is the highest-value screen and the one whose requirements are least knowable until real policies exist to bind — designing it in detail before then would be designing against imagination.

Risks worth stating

nexus is young. Its own README says it is "still under heavy development and is still missing many features". The product's simulation half rests on a substrate that may move underneath it, which is an argument for the UI treating the trainer as a replaceable job rather than a library.

The env grid may not be cheap. Sixteen live viewports onto a running sim is the one component whose cost is unknown. If readback contends with training throughput, it degrades to a sampled contact sheet at 2 Hz — still diagnostic, far cheaper.

Compose assumes a decomposition that may not hold. Some whole-body tasks resist a clean upper/lower split; the honest fallback is a single whole-body policy, and the screen should render that as one owner rather than pretending to a binding that is not there.
